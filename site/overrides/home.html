full-stack-agentic-engineer

Learn the LLM serving stack by building it

Topics of a few hours to a few days each, from GPUs to agents. Every topic goes from a primer to a from-scratch implementation to a complete one, tests what you learned with hands-on labs, and runs free on a laptop.

For anyone curious who wants to dig in and build deep skills. You need Python and patience, not a GPU.

How a topic works

A topic is one idea taken all the way down: theory first, then a version small enough to write yourself, then the complete thing. Here is one of them, 05-orchestrator/serving-orchestration, which decides which engine replica each request goes to and how many replicas run.

  1. 1 · Primer

    Theory, with worked numbers

    PRIMER.md: why round-robin fails for LLMs, routing signals, flow control, autoscaling, prefill/decode disaggregation, KV cache beyond HBM. Each formula comes with a worked number.

    Read it section by section, alongside step 2.

  2. 2 · Core

    A from-scratch implementation

    orchestrator-core: a discrete-event fleet simulator in the standard library only. Five fill-in notebooks and 57 tests. You write the routing and autoscaling decisions yourself.

    About 9 hours with the primer · T0

  3. 3 · Lab

    The complete implementation

    inference-gateway-lab: a real async router that makes the llm-d endpoint picker's decisions in front of fake or real vLLM servers, then Docker compose, kind and GKE Inference Gateway. Five notebooks and 76 tests.

    About 8 hours more · T0 to T3

Topics range from about an hour (the open-weight model landscape) to about 24 hours (the agent core, then the full agent-platform lab). Older topics in layers 00, 04, 06 and 07 follow the same arc with a primer and practice notebooks. The whole path is about 170 hours; the two shorter routes in the learning path take about 30 and 40.

You will be tested

Reading is not the end of a topic. Its notebooks set exercises, and a check cell under each one tells you whether your code is right.

  • Fill-in notebooks. Write the function where it says # YOUR CODE HERE; the check cell under it prints ✅ only when it passes. Worked answers sit apart, usually in solutions/.
  • Test suites. Most cores and labs ship pytest tests; in layers 01–05 they pin every number the primer computes.
  • Real deployments. Labs carry deploy/ targets: Docker compose, kind, any GPU box, and Google Cloud through Terraform.
  • Redo anything. git restore <notebook> returns an exercise to blank.

Where it runs, and what it costs

Every concept is learnable at T0. The other tiers turn predictions into measurements, and they are optional. A notebook that needs hardware you do not have runs a labelled offline path instead.

  • T0

    A laptop, Colab CPU or CI

    Free

    Simulators, calculators and from-scratch implementations. Add Docker for the local Kubernetes and compose stacks.

  • T1

    One small GPU

    Free on Colab or Kaggle, or about $0.3–0.7 an hour

    Real kernels, real vLLM with a 0.5–2B model, real metrics, on a T4 or a rented 24 GB card.

  • T2

    A multi-GPU box

    Free over PCIe on Kaggle; about $1–6 for an hour on two NVLink GPUs

    Collective bandwidth, peer-to-peer and NVLink, tensor parallelism. Rent it for about an hour.

  • T3

    Google Cloud, through Terraform

    Pay per use; needs a paid billing account

    How it looks in production on one cloud. Defaults to L4 Spot GPUs that scale to zero.

Prices are approximate as of 2026-09-26 and marked (verify) in COMPUTE.md, which also covers auto-stop and teardown habits.

The eight layers

The learning path does not climb them in order. It starts with the model, spirals through the engine and the hardware under it, and ends with the agents whose workloads shape every layer below.
